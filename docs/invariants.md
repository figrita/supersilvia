# Invariants, and what enforces them

[architecture.md](architecture.md) says what the system is. This says which of its claims are
**checked** and which rest on someone noticing — and it exists because those are different
kinds of risk, and only one of them is yours to carry.

A rule with a test behind it cannot rot quietly: it fails on the commit that breaks it, and
names the file. A rule that only lives in prose fails months later, on stage. The point of
the table below is that the second list is short, and that you know exactly what is on it.

## What only a person catches

Five rules, and nothing but attention stands behind them. They are the ones to spend review
on, because everything above is already spent.

**A `tick` never waits.** Not for a lock, not for a device, not for the GPU. Device threads
publish through a triple buffer, or a camera's one-frame slot whose lock `tick` only tries, and
`tick` reads the newest value and moves on. Nothing detects a blocking call added inside one;
the symptom is a dropped frame under load, on a machine that is not this one. *Read for: any
`.lock()`, `.recv()`, `.join()`, `.wait()`, or blocking I/O reachable from `CpuNode::tick`.*

**Nothing outside `nodes/` matches on a node slug.** Two bits of node semantics are allowed
out, both carried as data: `NodeDef::is_output`, read through `Node::def`, and
`PortDef::delayed`. A third would mean the graph knows what a `camera` is. *Read for: a string
literal naming a node, compared or matched anywhere but `nodes/`.* Reading or printing
`def.slug` is fine — the rule is about branching on its value.

**No shader module or pipeline is created on the synth or the frame thread**, but at
startup. A creation returns once the driver has compiled, which is the wait, and the synth
would stall a tick on it. `Program::create` runs on a `linker` thread (`render/link.rs`)
and a simulation's kernels on `sim-linker`; the mixer's, the viewers' and the renderer's own
stages are made once, with their owners. *Read for: a `create_shader_module`,
`create_render_pipeline` or `create_compute_pipeline` reachable from `Renderer::draw`, a
`Viewer` paint or `App::ui`.*

**A viewer holds its `Published` until after the `queue.submit` that carries its reads.**
The lease it keeps is what stops the synth drawing into an Output's or the mix's target it
blits, and so what puts the synth's next draw into that target after the blit on the one
queue. A viewer that dropped the `Published` before its blit was submitted would leave a read
the synth's draw is not ordered behind. The editor's lives on `App` until the next frame's
snapshot, past egui_wgpu's submit, and each `ViewerCallback` holds one until egui drops it
after that submit; a picture window drops its own after the submission before its present.
`tests/gpu_ring.rs` holds it with a viewer on another thread; a read race itself shows only
under the GPU's scheduling. *Read for: a `Published`, or a clone of the `Arc` holding one,
blitted from and dropped before the submission that carries the blit.*

**Nothing enumerates devices while drawing, or in a tick.** A `gst::DeviceMonitor` or a
PulseAudio round trip inside a panel is once a frame, which is sixty a second; it costs
milliseconds of frame time and buries the log. And one listing can take seconds — an ALSA card
that does not answer is waited for through the kernel's timeouts — so it runs on the Main
Input's `devices` thread, never the synth's, and both lists are handed in once it has answered.
*Read for: `capture_devices`, `audio::device::list`, or any `DeviceMonitor` reachable from a
`ui::` show function or from `Synth::step`.*

## Reading a change without reading Rust

The questions that catch a bad change here are not language questions. The rules above are the
first of them. The rest:

1. **Does the ownership match the drawing?** Someone who knows silvia knows which thing owns
   which. If a type in this codebase holds something that model says belongs to its parent,
   that is a finding. Ask *who owns this and who is
   allowed to change it*; the answer should be one thing and one thing.

2. **Did the behavior move?** The failure worth fearing is not ugly code — clippy and time
   handle ugly. It is a semantic quietly changed because the Rust shape was inconvenient: a
   clamp that moved, an order that became "whatever the map iterated", a degrade path that
   became an error path. Push on any behavior difference hard, including ones described as
   equivalent.

3. **Is the seam still one sentence?** If a module cannot be described without naming three
   others, the change put something in the wrong place. That is visible without reading a
   single type signature.

4. **Is the claim tested or asserted?** "This is fine because X" is a hypothesis. The table
   above is what turns one into a fact. A change that adds a rule and no way to check it has
   added a rule to the second list, and the second list should only ever get shorter.
